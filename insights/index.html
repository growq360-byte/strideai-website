Aging and mobility: why everyday walking data matters

Brief, sources checked November 20, 2026

A single walk in the clinic is not the same as daily life. A 2026 meta-analysis of 11 studies found that supervised lab gait speed averaged 0.22 m/s faster than real-world gait speed measured with wearables, and called for more standardized real-world methods [1].

Continuous in-shoe measurement during everyday walking is one way to collect that kind of data. StrideAI records everyday walking rhythm and time on feet for general wellness and for research partners' own studies. It is not a fall-risk or diagnostic tool.

Sources (1)
  1. Nguyen TML et al. Laboratory vs daily-life gait speed in healthy older adults: systematic review and meta-analysis. Gait & Posture, 2026. PMID 42296946. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gaitpost.2026.110254

Sport and athlete load: measuring where load meets the ground

Brief, sources checked November 20, 2026

Running injuries are common: a meta-analysis found 17.8 running-related injuries per 1,000 hours for novice runners and 7.7 for recreational runners [1]. An international consensus statement on load in sport gives guidance on prescribing and monitoring training load, competition load and athlete well-being [2].

Teams already rely on wearables, but the data does not always lead to decisions. In a survey of high-level football clubs, all responding clubs used GPS and heart-rate monitors in every training session, yet club staff rated monitoring less effective than they expected; the top barriers were limited staff and coach buy-in [3]. A 2024 survey of 72 team-sport performance staff found 87.5% use wearable data to plan training but only 50% use it for competition decisions [4].

Most team systems are worn on the torso, while the load of every stride passes through the foot. A footwear layer that measures pressure and motion at the ground can add context coaches can read. StrideAI's role today is data acquisition, with models on the roadmap. StrideAI records training load as a training and research input. It does not assess injury risk or diagnose injuries.

Military training: the lower-limb readiness problem

Brief, sources checked November 20, 2026

Initial entry training is one of the most demanding running and marching settings anywhere. A U.S. Army review of calendar year 2016 counted 65,026 injuries among 106,367 trainees; mechanical energy to the lower limbs caused 75% of all injuries, and 65% of those came from cumulative microtrauma, meaning overuse [1].

In a prospective cohort of 2,988 Army Basic Combat Training trainees (2017 to 2023), 1,487 had at least one coded musculoskeletal injury, about half the cohort [2]. In British Army basic training (2015 to 2016), women in officer training had 114 stress fractures per 1,000 versus 19 per 1,000 for men [3].

The Army's Holistic Health and Fitness (H2F) program embeds performance teams in units and, as of December 2025, plans to reach 129 active-duty brigades plus Guard and Reserve formations by FY2029 [4]. Objective foot-level load data fits naturally into human performance programs as a training and research input. StrideAI records training load as a training and research input. It does not assess injury risk or diagnose injuries.

Workplace: jobs done on your feet

Brief, sources checked November 20, 2026

U.S. private industry employers reported 2.5 million nonfatal workplace injuries and illnesses in 2024 [1].

Two categories stand out for people who work on their feet. Overexertion, repetitive motion and bodily conditions accounted for 946,290 cases involving days away, restricted work or job transfer across 2023 to 2024, about 32% of such cases. Falls, slips and trips accounted for 721,720 such cases, including 479,480 with days away from work [1].

A NIOSH review discusses health effects of prolonged standing at work [2]. StrideAI records standing time, steps and load as activity data for workplace wellness programs. StrideAI records training load as a training and research input. It does not assess injury risk or diagnose injuries.

Sources (2)

  2. Waters TR, Dick RB (NIOSH). Health risks of prolonged standing at work and intervention effectiveness. Rehabil Nurs, 2015. PMID 25041875. https://doi.org/10.1002/rnj.166

Smart footwear and insole market: size, drivers and lessons

Brief, sources checked November 20, 2026

Market estimates vary widely. Future Market Insights values the smart insole market at USD 210.0 million in 2025, projecting USD 1,128.3 million by 2036 [1]. Fortune Business Insights values smart shoes at USD 549.13 million in 2025, reaching USD 2,011.86 million by 2034 [2]. Grand View Research projected smart shoes at USD 1,103.2 million by 2030 [3]. Scopes, base years and methods differ, so these figures show direction, not a precise size.

Named drivers include endurance sports analytics and the move from lab force plates to wearables [1].

Earlier attempts show the risks. NURVV Limited, maker of NURVV Run insoles, was dissolved in January 2025 [4]. Under Armour connected 1 million shoes to its MapMyRun app, but its plan for app users to drive apparel sales did not pan out [5]. The lesson: data has to drive a decision that a specific buyer values.
